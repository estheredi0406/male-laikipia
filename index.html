The question

From a student plan to an investable case

Laikipia's conservancies protect some of Kenya's most important wildlife, yet most still depend on one source of income: tourism. In 2025 I spent a month there with ALU's School of Wildlife Conservation and wrote a plan for Male Ranch Conservancy, a family-owned property, to earn from tourism, regenerative grazing, products made from the invasive Opuntia cactus and, later, carbon.

A year later I rebuilt the finances from scratch, the way a funder would read them: every number traced to a source, upfront costs separated from running costs, and each stage matched with the kind of capital that fits it.

Chapter 01 · The original plan, 2025

A conservancy that earns beyond tourism

Male Ranch already has visible wildlife and a tourism draw. My plan proposed three enterprises that work with the land instead of against it, with carbon credits as a later layer.

Pillar 1

Eco and research tourism

Low-impact, full-immersion camping for solo travellers, students and researchers, with Mpala Research Centre as a partner.

  • Bush survival camps
  • Starbed platforms
  • Community camping circles
  • Eco-research camps
Pillar 2

Regenerative grazing

Holistic, rotational grazing modelled on the Northern Rangelands Trust and Ol Pejeta, so cattle restore grassland instead of degrading it.

  • Biomass and soil health monitoring
  • Groundwater and vegetation tracking
  • Meat and dairy for local markets
Pillar 3

Opuntia products

Turning the invasive cactus into value. Local people are paid to bring it in, and it is processed instead of spread.

  • Food: jams, juice, flour
  • Cosmetics: oils and gels
  • Bioenergy: biogas, fertiliser
Revisited · 2026

Five things I would change

01
Then

Infrastructure was a yearly running cost that grew each year.

Separate upfront investment from running costs

Camps, solar, water, fencing and a processing unit must be paid for before the first guest arrives. That creates a funding need the original plan never showed.

02
Then

Each stream had a total "based on comparable sites".

Build revenue from volumes and prices

Beds × occupancy × nightly rate. Jars per week × price. Animals × weight × price per kilo. Each price is taken from a cited source in Laikipia or Kenya.

03
Then

Carbon credits earned from Year 2, partly from cactus.

Treat carbon as upside, not base case

Baselines, validation and verification take years and cost money upfront. Kenya also requires communities to share carbon revenue, and nearby projects face legal challenges. The base case now excludes carbon.

04
Then

No funding plan, because the plan never ran out of cash.

Match each kind of money to each stage

Grants for training, baselines and governance. A concessional loan for camps and equipment. Revenue for operations. Outcome payments, such as carbon, for measured results.

05
Then

One scenario, with everything going to plan.

Stress-test against bad years

A drought in Year 2, lower occupancy and weaker product sales. A plan worth funding should show what happens when things go wrong.

Governance

Risks I flagged in 2025

Family ownership. Conflicting visions among family owners, addressed through facilitated governance meetings and binding internal agreements.

Land rights. Boundary and access disputes, addressed through participatory mapping and formal titles.

Unequal participation. Women and youth represented in jobs and leadership, with benefits paid directly to households.

Policy alignment. Conservancy goals linked to county and national climate, tourism and development policy.

Chapter 04 · About

Hi, I'm Esther.

I build things with data and I like knowing how the money works. I'm finishing a software engineering degree at the African Leadership University, where I focus on data analysis, and I spend the rest of my time running operations for a small marketplace in Kigali.

Male Ranch is the project that pulled those two sides together. I wrote the first plan as a student in Laikipia. Coming back to it a year later, I wanted to answer the questions I couldn't answer then: what would this actually cost, who would pay for each part, and what happens in a bad year?

What this project taught me

Three lessons from rebuilding it

01

Money has a shape

A camp, a training course and a carbon credit are not funded the same way. Grants, loans and revenue each fit a different moment, and getting that wrong can sink a good project.

02

Every number needs a source

My first plan had totals that felt right. Rebuilding it from nightly rates, jar prices and beef prices showed me where my instincts were optimistic.

03

People hold it together

The community stream earns the least, but without it the land disputes and unequal benefits would put every other stream at risk.
